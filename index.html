PID Playground

Why PID controllers matter

A PID controller keeps something at a target automatically: it measures how far off it is, and adjusts the push to close the gap. It's the most widely used controller in engineering, in everything from factories to the devices around you.

  • Cars: cruise control holds your speed, uphill or down.
  • Drones: they stay level and hold their height in the wind.
  • Heating and cooling: thermostats keep a room at one temperature.
  • Robotics: motors move robot arms to exact positions.
  • 3D printing: the nozzle stays at the right melting temperature.
  • Electrical engineering: power supplies hold a steady voltage while the load changes.

Guide: how this works

What this app does

It simulates a real system (a car, a motor, or a mass on a spring) being steered toward a target by a PID controller, the most common controller in engineering. You turn the controller's three dials and watch what happens.

The three dials

Think of cruise control deciding how hard to press the gas.

  • Kp (proportional): "How far am I from the target?" Far away means push hard; close means push gently. On its own it usually stops a little short of the target.
  • Ki (integral): "How long have I been behind?" It keeps adding push while there's still a gap, which closes the leftover gap. Too much makes the system overshoot.
  • Kd (derivative): "How fast am I closing in?" It eases off early when the system is rushing toward the target, like a brake on overshooting.

How it all fits together

The three dials aren't separate: each one fixes a weakness of another, and each one can cause a problem that another one fixes.

  • Kp does most of the work, but leaves a gap. More Kp gets there faster, but it overshoots more, and it still stops a little short of the target.
  • Ki closes the gap Kp leaves, but because it keeps adding push, it tends to make the overshoot worse.
  • Kd calms the overshoot that Kp and Ki cause, by easing off early.

A simple tuning recipe: raise Kp until it responds quickly; if it overshoots, add Kd; if it stops short of the target, add a little Ki. Then nudge all three until you're happy.

The numbers

  • Overshoot: how far it goes past the target before coming back.
  • Rise time: how quickly it gets most of the way there.
  • Settling time: when it finally stays close to where it ends up.
  • Steady-state error: the gap left over at the end. Ki is what removes it.
  • Peak effort: the hardest the controller pushed, compared with what real hardware can give.

Real-world effects

  • Real-world limit: real engines and power supplies can only push so hard, like a gas pedal that hits the floor.
  • Windup and anti-windup: see the next section.
  • Sensor noise: real sensors flicker a little. The controller only sees the flickery reading (the thin gray line), and Kd overreacts to it.
  • Derivative filter: smooths the flicker before Kd reacts, so the push stays calm.
  • Hill (car only): partway through, the road turns uphill and gravity pulls the car back. Kp alone leaves it slower for good; Ki keeps adding push until it's back at the target speed.
  • Headwind (car only): a wind starts blowing against the car, adding drag. Like the hill, it's a steady push that only Ki fully makes up.
  • Wind gust (drone only): a downdraft shoves the drone down for a moment, then stops. It's over before Ki can do much, so the dip depends on how hard and fast Kp and Kd push back.
  • Delay (car only): the engine acts on each command late. A strong controller then wobbles; see the section below.
  • Heavy load (car only): 500 kg of passengers and luggage. The same push speeds a heavier car up more slowly, but it still ends at the same speed.
  • Package pickup (drone only): partway through, the drone picks up a 0.5 kg package. It's heavier now, so the thrust that held it up isn't enough and it sinks. Like the hill, it's a steady pull that only Ki fully makes up.
  • Sticky friction (car only): a stopped car needs a hard enough push to start rolling, so a weak controller leaves it stuck; see the section below.

Windup, with a frustration meter

Think of Ki as a frustration meter: the longer the car is behind its target, the fuller the meter gets, and the fuller it is, the harder the controller presses the gas.

The car starts slow, so the meter fills: "I'm behind… still behind…" Soon the gas pedal is on the floor (the real-world limit). What happens next is the difference between windup and anti-windup:

Without anti-windup: windup happens (the problem)

  1. The pedal is on the floor, but the meter keeps filling anyway.
  2. The car reaches the target with the meter overfull.
  3. So the controller keeps flooring it, and the car overshoots.

With anti-windup: the fix

  1. The pedal is on the floor, so the meter freezes.
  2. The car reaches the target with the meter not overfull.
  3. So the controller eases off in time: little or no overshoot.

In one line: windup is the meter overfilling while the pedal can't go any further; anti-windup stops it from overfilling.

See it yourself: pick Car cruise control, set Ki to about 100 and note the Overshoot. Then open Real-world effects and turn on Anti-windup: the overshoot drops.

The delay, with a shower

You know a shower where the water takes a few seconds to change temperature after you turn the knob? That gap is a delay, and it's exactly what happens to the car when the engine acts on each command late.

  1. The water is too cold, so you turn the knob hotter. Nothing changes yet.
  2. Still cold, so you turn it even hotter.
  3. The hot water finally arrives, far too hot. You turn it way back, and soon it's too cold again.

You're reacting to old news: the water you feel is from a few seconds ago. The controller does the same with the car's speed, so it overcorrects back and forth, and the car wobbles.

Strong Kp with a delay: wobbles (the problem)

  1. The car is slow, so the controller pushes hard, but the engine acts late.
  2. By the time the push arrives, the car is already near the target.
  3. It shoots past, the controller pulls back hard, also late: the car swings back and forth.

Gentler Kp, or adding Kd: calm

  1. Smaller corrections (a gentler Kp) don't overshoot as far.
  2. Kd eases off as soon as the car is speeding up fast, before it reaches the target.
  3. Either way, the swings die out and the car settles.

In one line: with a delay, the controller reacts to where the car was, so pushing too hard makes it overcorrect back and forth.

See it yourself: pick Car cruise control, open Real-world effects, turn on Delay, and drag Kp up to about 1500: the car wobbles. Then raise Kd to about 500: it calms down.

Sticky friction, with a heavy couch

Think of pushing a heavy couch across the floor. A gentle push does nothing at all. You have to shove hard enough to break it free, and once it's sliding, it takes less effort to keep it going. That's sticky friction, and the car has it too: 400 N to get rolling, then 150 N resisting it while it moves.

Too weak a push: stuck (the problem)

  1. With a small Kp, the controller only pushes gently.
  2. The push never reaches the 400 N it takes to get the car rolling.
  3. So the car never moves, even though the controller is trying.

A stronger Kp, or Ki: breaks free

  1. A bigger Kp pushes harder right away and beats the 400 N.
  2. Or Ki keeps adding push while the car sits still (the frustration meter filling up) until it's enough.
  3. Either way, the car breaks free and starts rolling.

In one line: sticky friction holds a stopped car in place until the push is strong enough to break it free.

See it yourself: pick Car cruise control, open Real-world effects, turn on Sticky friction, and drag Kp down to 10: the car stays stuck. Then raise Ki to about 5: after a moment it breaks free.
