Guide: how this works

What this app does

It simulates a real system (a car, a motor, or a mass on a spring) being steered toward a target by a PID controller, the most common controller in engineering. You turn the controller's three dials and watch what happens.

The three dials

Think of cruise control deciding how hard to press the gas.

  • Kp (proportional): "How far am I from the target?" Far away means push hard; close means push gently. On its own it usually stops a little short of the target.
  • Ki (integral): "How long have I been behind?" It keeps adding push while there's still a gap, which closes the leftover gap. Too much makes the system overshoot.
  • Kd (derivative): "How fast am I closing in?" It eases off early when the system is rushing toward the target, like a brake on overshooting.

The picture and the chart

The picture replays the simulation: the blue label is what's measured (like the car's speed), and the orange label and arrow are what the controller sets (like the engine force). It turns red when the controller hits a real limit. The chart shows the same run over time: the blue curve is the system, the dashed line is the target, and the dot marks the moment the picture is showing.

The numbers

  • Overshoot: how far it goes past the target before coming back.
  • Rise time: how quickly it gets most of the way there.
  • Settling time: when it finally stays close to where it ends up.
  • Steady-state error: the gap left over at the end. Ki is what removes it.
  • Peak effort: the hardest the controller pushed, compared with what real hardware can give.

The challenge

Each system has a target to hit, like a homework problem. Every goal shows ✓ or ✗ as you tune, and you've won when it says Spec met!

Real-world effects

  • Real-world limit: real engines and power supplies can only push so hard, like a gas pedal that hits the floor.
  • Windup and anti-windup: see the next section.
  • Sensor noise: real sensors flicker a little. The controller only sees the flickery reading (the thin gray line), and Kd overreacts to it.
  • Derivative filter: smooths the flicker before Kd reacts, so the push stays calm.

Windup, with a frustration meter

Think of Ki as a frustration meter: the longer the car is behind its target, the fuller the meter gets, and the fuller it is, the harder the controller presses the gas.

The car starts slow, so the meter fills: "I'm behind… still behind…" Soon the gas pedal is on the floor (the real-world limit). What happens next is the difference between windup and anti-windup:

Without anti-windup: windup happens (the problem)

  1. The pedal is on the floor, but the meter keeps filling anyway.
  2. The car reaches the target with the meter overfull.
  3. So the controller keeps flooring it, and the car overshoots.

With anti-windup: the fix

  1. The pedal is on the floor, so the meter freezes.
  2. The car reaches the target with the meter not overfull.
  3. So the controller eases off in time: little or no overshoot.

In one line: windup is the meter overfilling while the pedal can't go any further; anti-windup stops it from overfilling.

See it yourself: pick Car cruise control, set Ki to about 100 and note the Overshoot. Then open Real-world effects and turn on Anti-windup: the overshoot drops.

Try this first

  1. Pick Car cruise control and drag Kp up: faster, but it stops short of the target.
  2. Raise Ki: the gap closes, but watch the overshoot.
  3. Open Real-world effects and turn on anti-windup: the overshoot drops.
